\begin{abstract}
%% Text of abstract
\texttt{acousticHelmholtzFoam} is a parallel finite-volume solver for frequency-domain acoustic wave propagation in heterogeneous two-phase media. The solver is implemented in OpenFOAM and solves the complex Helmholtz equation in terms of coupled real and imaginary pressure fields. Perfectly matched layers are used to truncate open domains, and the resulting block-coupled linear system is assembled and solved through PETSc with MUMPS. This paper describes the mathematical model, the finite-volume implementation, the PML field setup, and verification cases for piston radiation and one-dimensional two-phase acoustic propagation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


\label{sec:intro}

Time-harmonic acoustic fields are commonly described by the Helmholtz equation when the acoustic source operates at a prescribed frequency and the transient wave propagation is not the primary quantity of interest. This frequency-domain formulation is useful in resonators, transducer-driven systems, acoustic radiation problems, and acoustic manipulation devices, because it gives direct access to the pressure amplitude, phase, velocity amplitude, and radiation-pressure-related quantities without resolving many periods of the underlying wave motion \cite{morse1986theoretical,bruus2012acoustofluidicsPT}. In two-phase systems, however, the acoustic problem becomes more demanding: the density and speed of sound may change by orders of magnitude across gas-liquid interfaces, and the pressure field can be strongly affected by reflection, transmission, and resonance at these material jumps.

One motivating application is acoustic levitation of liquid droplets. In single-axis levitators, the standing or partially standing acoustic field determines the radiation pressure and momentum flux acting on the droplet, and therefore influences the droplet position, deformation, and stability \cite{shi1996deformation,yarin1998acoustic,xie2001parametric,andrade2010finite}. Predictive simulations of such systems require an acoustic solver that can represent heterogeneous media and interface-resolved material fields while remaining compatible with two-phase flow solvers. In a finite-volume environment this is particularly attractive, because the same mesh and volume-fraction field used for interface-capturing or interface-tracking methods can also be used to define the acoustic material properties \cite{Brackbill1992,Popinet2018,Liu2023incons}.

Open-domain acoustic simulations also require a robust treatment of outgoing waves. Radiation boundary conditions can be effective in special geometries or for waves with known propagation directions, but they are less convenient for general three-dimensional fields and near-field radiation problems. Perfectly matched layers (PMLs) provide a more flexible domain-truncation strategy by attenuating outgoing waves in an artificial absorbing layer while minimizing reflections at the interface between the physical domain and the layer \cite{Kaltenbacher2013modified}. For a finite-volume implementation in OpenFOAM, the PML formulation must be expressed in terms of operators and coefficient fields that can be assembled efficiently on arbitrary meshes.

This paper presents \texttt{acousticHelmholtzFoam}, a parallel OpenFOAM solver for frequency-domain acoustic waves in heterogeneous two-phase media. The complex Helmholtz equation is split into coupled real and imaginary pressure equations, producing a real-valued block system. The finite-volume operators are assembled in OpenFOAM, while the global block-coupled linear system is solved using PETSc with MUMPS. A companion utility, \texttt{setPMLFields}, generates the scalar and directional tensor PML coefficient fields used by the solver for rectangular and spherical absorbing layers. The solver also computes derived fields such as acoustic velocity components, pressure amplitude, radiation pressure, and momentum flux, which are useful for acoustic levitation and related multiphysics applications.

The main contributions of the software are: (i) a finite-volume formulation of the heterogeneous Helmholtz equation for two-phase material fields, (ii) a PML implementation based on precomputed OpenFOAM scalar and tensor fields, (iii) a block-coupled PETSc/MUMPS solution procedure for the real and imaginary pressure components, and (iv) verification cases for piston radiation and one-dimensional two-phase wave propagation. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. \Cref{sec:model} derives the acoustic model and PML formulation. \Cref{sec:implementation} describes the finite-volume implementation and block-coupled solution strategy. \Cref{sec:usage} summarizes the program workflow and required input fields. \Cref{sec:verification} presents verification cases, while \Cref{sec:application} outlines the intended application to single-axis acoustic levitation.


\section{Mathematical model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Wave equation for heterogeneous medium}
The governing equations of fluid motion for density $\rho$, pressure $p$, velocity $\v$  are expressed as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item The mass conservation equation:
        \begin{equation}
            \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho \v)=0.
            \label{eq:massConserv}
        \end{equation}
        \item The momentum conservation equation (neglecting gravitational/viscous effects):
        \begin{equation}
            \frac{\partial \rho \v}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho \v\v)=-\nabla p.
            \label{eq:momConserv}    
        \end{equation}
        \item The equation of state
        \begin{equation}
            \rho  =\rho(p,s),
        \end{equation}
        with the specific entropy $s$.
\end{itemize}  
   
Consider a quiescent flow ($\v_0=\mathbf{0}$) characterized by a spatial dependent density $\rho_0(\x)$  prior to the introduction of any acoustic disturbance. The classical perturbation theory is employed to describe the propagation of acoustic waves, wherein the acoustic field is represented as small perturbations (denoted by the subscript $1$, with $|\rho_1|\ll \rho_0$ and $|p_1|\ll p_0$) in the density, pressure, and velocity fields:
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
            \rho &= \rho_0 + \rho_1, \\
            p &= p_0 + p_1, \\
            \v &= \v_1.
            \label{eq:1stPert}
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

For unperturbed fluid is $\v=0$, it follows from \cref{eq:momConserv} $\nabla p_0=0$.
    
Substituting \cref{eq:1stPert} into \cref{eq:momConserv}, by neglecting the high order terms (e.g., $\rho_1\v_1$, $\v_1\v_1$) yield
        \begin{equation}
            \rho_0 \frac{\partial \v_1}{\partial t}=-\nabla p_1.
            \label{eq:1stMomConserv}            
        \end{equation}

After introducing the total derivative $\frac{d}{dt}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \v \cdot \nabla$ The \cref{eq:massConserv} can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d \rho}{dt} + \rho \nabla\cdot\v =0 
\end{equation}

While the propagation of ist sound a adiabatic process, means  $\frac{d s}{d t}=0$, and we have 
\begin{equation}
     \frac{d \rho}{dt} =\left( \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p}\right)_s\frac{d p}{\partial t} + \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial s}\right)_p\frac{d s}{d t} = \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p}\frac{d p}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{c^2}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} + (\v\cdot\nabla)p\right)
\end{equation}
Where we have use the defintion of the sound speed. The mass conservation now becomes 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} + (\v\cdot\nabla)p + \rho c^2 \, \nabla\cdot\v = 0
    \label{eq:pMassConti}
\end{equation}

Following the same procedure, we use our first order pertubation ansatz \cref{eq:1stPert}, by neglecting the high order terms it follows
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial p_1}{\partial t} = -\rho_0 c^2\, \nabla \cdot \v_1.
    \label{eq:1stMassConserv}
\end{equation}
Taking time derivative then use \cref{eq:1stMomConserv} to eliminate $\v$, we have
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial^2 p_1}{\partial t^2}  = -\rho_0 c^2\, \nabla \cdot \frac{\partial \v_1}{ \partial t} =\rho_0 c^2\, \nabla \cdot\left(\frac{\nabla p_1}{\rho_0}\right),
    \label{eq:heteWaveP}
\end{equation}
which is the pressure wave equation for heterogeneous medium. Taken $\rho_0$ as constant, than we get the familiar form of the wave equation for homogeneous medium
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial^2 p_1}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \Delta p_1
\end{equation}

\subsection{Helmholtz equation}
If the acoustic system is driven by single frequency and the timescale is much larger then the period of the sound wave. The acoustic pressure field can be written in the following time harmonic form
\begin{equation}
    p_1(x,t) = \mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\omega t}P(x))
    \label{as:timeHarmonic}
\end{equation}
Where the complex pressure $P(x)=P_{re}+ iP_{im}(x)$ is spatial function. We substitute \cref{as:timeHarmonic} into the general wave equation \cref{eq:heteWaveP} , than it follows
\begin{equation}
    \rho_0\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla P}{\rho_0}\right) + k^2P=0,
    \label{eq:heteHelmholtz}
\end{equation}
where $k=\frac{\omega}{c}$ is the wave number. 

\subsection{Perfect Matched Layer}
As the simulation domain must be closed, it could be tricky if the acoustic system to be simulated is not closed, such as the sound wave from a speaker that travels far away. For this reason, we must modify the "open boundary" with some sort of boundary condition so we can enclose the simulation domain. For the case where the outgoing wave has a specific form or fixed direction a absorbing or radiation boundary condition could be a simple  way to model the open boundary. While for more general cases, the Perfect Matched Layer(PML) is by far the most effective boundary condition for the open boundary.

By PML, the simulation domain is extended with a additional layer at the open boundary, through the modification of the helmholtz equation the outgoing wave will goes into this layer without reflection (where the name PML comes from) and be fully absorbed inside it. This make it behaves like a infinity extended area. 

 The Helmholtz  equation is modified by extending the real spatial coordinate $\x$ into a complex space, defined as $\tilde{\x} = \x + i\h(\x)$. Under this transformation, \cref{eq:heteHelmholtz} becomes
\begin{equation}
               \rho_0 \tilde\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\tilde\nabla P}{\rho_0}\right) + k^2P=0, 
              \label{eq:stretchedHelmholtz}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\nabla}$ denotes the Laplacian operator in the stretched coordinate system. To illustrate how the auxiliary function $h(x)$ looks like, we consider the domain with constant $\rho_0$, than the corresponding plane traveling wave solution to \cref{eq:stretchedHelmholtz} is 
 \begin{equation*}
   P = |P| \exp(i\k \cdot \tilde \x)=|P| \exp(-\k \cdot \h(\x)) \exp(i\k\cdot \x).  
 \end{equation*}
 Where the wave vector $\k$ has direction of the travelling wave and length of the wave number.  $\h(\x)$ is designed such that it introduces no artificial damping within the interior region while providing attenuation in the PML region, which means, 
\begin{equation*}
            \k\cdot \h(\x) = \left\{
            \begin{array}{ll}
                > 0 & x \in \Omega^{PML} \\
                = 0 & x \in \Omega / \Omega^{PML}
            \end{array}
            \right.
        \end{equation*}
 
 Building upon the fundamental principles of the PML formulation described above, we derive here the specific implementation proposed by Kaltenbacher et al.\cite{Kaltenbacher2013modified}. Following \citep{Kaltenbacher2013modified}, the complex coordinate transformation is defined as $i\h(\x) = \frac{1}{i\omega}\int \boldsymbol{\sigma}(\x) \,dx$, where $\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\x)$ is the damping vector composed of $\sigma_x(x)$, $\sigma_y(y)$, and $\sigma_z(z)$. Accordingly, the following relations hold: 
 \begin{equation}
     \begin{aligned}
         \frac{\partial \tilde{x}}{\partial x} & = \eta_x=1+\frac{\sigma_x}{i\omega},\quad  \frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{x}}=\frac{\rho_0}{\eta_x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x},\\
         \frac{\partial \tilde{y}}{\partial y} & = \eta_y=1+\frac{\sigma_y}{i\omega},\quad  \frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{y}}=\frac{\rho_0}{\eta_y}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}, \\
         \frac{\partial \tilde{z}}{\partial z} & = \eta_z=1+\frac{\sigma_z}{i\omega},\quad  \frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{z}}=\frac{\rho_0}{\eta_z}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}. 
     \end{aligned}
     \label{eq:dampEtaDef}
 \end{equation}
 Substituting these expressions into \cref{eq:stretchedHelmholtz} states 
 \begin{equation}
     -\frac{(i\omega)^2}{c^2}P + \frac{\rho_0}{\eta_x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_0 \eta_x}\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\right) + \frac{\rho_0}{\eta_y}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_0 \eta_y}\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) +\frac{\rho_0}{\eta_z}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_0 \eta_z}\frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\right)=0.
     \label{eq:extendedStretchHelmholz}
 \end{equation}
 Since $\eta_x$, $\eta_y$, and $\eta_z$ depend solely on $x$, $y$, and $z$, respectively, multiplying both sides of \cref{eq:extendedStretchHelmholz} by $\eta_x\eta_y\eta_z$ leads to the reformulated expression:
 \begin{equation}
     -\eta_x\eta_y\eta_z \frac{(i\omega)^2}{c^2 \rho_0}P + \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\eta_y\eta_z}{\rho_0\eta_x}\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\eta_x\eta_z}{\rho_0\eta_y}\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) +\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{\eta_x\eta_y}{\rho_0\eta_z}\frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\right)=0.
     \label{eq:reformedStretchHelmholz}
 \end{equation}
Expanding the first term using \cref{eq:dampEtaDef} gives 
 \begin{equation}
   \begin{aligned}
      \eta_x\eta_y\eta_z\frac{(i\omega)^2}{c^2}P &= \frac{(i\omega)^2}{c^2}\left(1+\frac{\sigma_x}{i\omega}\right)\left(1+\frac{\sigma_y}{i\omega}\right)\left(1+\frac{\sigma_z}{i\omega}\right)P \\
      &= \frac{1}{c^2}\left((i\omega)^2 + i\omega(\sigma_x+\sigma_y+\sigma_z) + \sigma_x\sigma_y + \sigma_x\sigma_z +\sigma_y\sigma_z + \frac{\sigma_x\sigma_y\sigma_z}{i\omega}\right)P  
 \end{aligned} 
 \label{eq:1stTermExt}
 \end{equation}
 
 We therefore proceed to separate \cref{eq:reformedStretchHelmholz} into its real and imaginary components. 

 Expanding first term in \cref{eq:dampEtaDef} yields 
 \begin{equation}
   \begin{aligned}
      \eta_x\eta_y\eta_z\frac{(i\omega)^2}{c^2}P &= \frac{(i\omega)^2}{c^2}\left(1+\frac{\sigma_x}{i\omega}\right)\left(1+\frac{\sigma_y}{i\omega}\right)\left(1+\frac{\sigma_z}{i\omega}\right)(P_{re} + iP_{im}) \\
      %&= \frac{1}{c^2}\left((i\omega)^2 + i\omega(\sigma_x+\sigma_y+\sigma_z) + \sigma_x\sigma_y + \sigma_x\sigma_z +\sigma_y\sigma_z + \frac{\sigma_x\sigma_y\sigma_z}{i\omega}\right)(P_{re} + iP_{im}) \\
      &=\frac{1}{c^2}
\left[ (AP_{re} - BP_{im})
+ i(AP_{im} + BP_{re}) \right], \quad \text{with} \\
A &= -\omega^2 + \sigma_x\sigma_y + \sigma_x\sigma_z + \sigma_y\sigma_z,\\
B &= \omega(\sigma_x+\sigma_y+\sigma_z) - \frac{\sigma_x\sigma_y\sigma_z}{\omega}
 \end{aligned} 
% \label{eq:1stTermExt}
 \end{equation}
 
Applying the same expansion to the second term in \cref{eq:dampEtaDef} gives
 \begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
     \frac{\eta_y\eta_z}{\eta_x}\frac{\partial P}{\partial x} &= \frac{\left(1+\frac{\sigma_y}{i\omega}\right)\left(1+\frac{\sigma_z}{i\omega}\right)}{\left(1+\frac{\sigma_x}{i\omega}\right)}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(P_{re} + iP_{im}) \\
     &= \left(A\frac{\partial P_{re}}{\partial x} - B\frac{\partial P_{im}}{\partial x}\right)
+ i\left(A\frac{\partial P_{im}}{\partial x} + B\frac{\partial P_{re}}{\partial x}\right) \quad \text{with} \\
A&=1 + \frac{\sigma_x(\sigma_y+\sigma_z -\sigma_x) - \sigma_y\sigma_z }{(\omega^2 + \sigma^2(x))} ,\\
B&= -\frac{(\sigma_x\sigma_y\sigma_z)/\omega + \omega(\sigma_y+\sigma_z-\sigma_x)}{(\omega^2 + \sigma^2_x)}.
    \end{aligned}
 \end{equation}
 The same procedure is applied to the third and fourth terms in \cref{eq:dampEtaDef}. Collecting all contributions yields the PML formulation of wave propagation in the frequency domain:
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{aligned}
                \frac{\omega^2}{c^2} P_{re}  +  \rho_0 \nabla \cdot\left(\frac{1}{\rho_0} \nabla P_{re}\right) - C_0 P_{re} + C_1 P_{im}   
               + \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{T}_0 \cdot \nabla P_{re} + \mathbf{T}_1 \cdot \nabla P_{im}) &= 0, \\
            \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}P_{im} +  \rho_0 \nabla \cdot\left(\frac{1}{\rho_0} \nabla P_{im}\right)  - C_0 P_{im} - C_1 P_{re}
             + \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{T}_0 \cdot \nabla P_{im} - \mathbf{T}_1 \cdot \nabla P_{re}) &= 0, 
            \end{aligned}
            \label{eq:helmholtzPML}
        \end{equation*}
where $C_0$, $C_1$ and the tensor coefficients $\mathbf{T}_0$, $\mathbf{T}_1$ are defined as follows:
\begin{paracol}{2}
      \begin{equation*}
      %\footnotesize
        \begin{aligned}
            C_0 &= \frac{\sigma_x\sigma_y + \sigma_x\sigma_z + \sigma_y\sigma_z}{c^2} \\%[1ex]
            T_{0xx} &= \frac{\sigma_x(\sigma_y+\sigma_z-\sigma_x) - \sigma_y\sigma_z}{(\omega^2 + \sigma^2(x))}, \\
            T_{0yy} &= \frac{\sigma_y(\sigma_x+\sigma_z-\sigma_y)-\sigma_x\sigma_z}{(\omega^2 + \sigma^2(y))}, \\
            T_{0zz} &= \frac{\sigma_z(\sigma_x+\sigma_y-\sigma_z)-\sigma_x\sigma_y}{(\omega^2 + \sigma^2(z))}, \\
            \mathbf{T_0} &= \begin{pmatrix}
              T_{0xx} & 0 & 0 \\
             0 &  T_{0yy} & 0 \\
             0 & 0 &  T_{0zz}  
            \end{pmatrix} 
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}
    \switchcolumn
       \begin{equation*}
      % \footnotesize
        \begin{aligned}
            C_1 &= \frac{\omega(\sigma_x+\sigma_y+\sigma_z) - (\sigma_x\sigma_y\sigma_z)/\omega}{c^2} \\%[1ex]
            T_{1xx} &= \frac{(\sigma_x\sigma_y\sigma_z)/\omega + \omega(\sigma_y+\sigma_z-\sigma_x)}{(\omega^2 + \sigma^2(x))}, \\
            T_{1yy} &= \frac{(\sigma_x\sigma_y\sigma_z)/\omega + \omega(\sigma_x+\sigma_z-\sigma_y)}{(\omega^2 + \sigma^2(y))}, \\
            T_{1zz} &= \frac{(\sigma_x\sigma_y\sigma_z)/\omega + \omega(\sigma_x+\sigma_y-\sigma_z)}{(\omega^2 + \sigma^2(z))}, \\
            \mathbf{T_1} &= \begin{pmatrix}
              T_{1xx} & 0 & 0 \\
             0 &  T_{1yy} & 0 \\
             0 & 0 &  T_{1zz}   
            \end{pmatrix} 
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}
\end{paracol}

\section{Numerical implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

\subsection{Solver overview}
\label{sec:solverOverview}
\texttt{acousticHelmholtzFoam} is implemented as an OpenFOAM user application for
frequency-domain acoustic simulations on finite-volume meshes.  The unknown
complex pressure is stored as two real-valued volume fields, \texttt{Pre} and
\texttt{Pim}.  The solver reads the computational mesh, the initial pressure
fields, the material indicator \texttt{alpha.water}, and the acoustic parameters
from \texttt{constant/transportProperties}.  The dictionary provides the gas and
liquid densities \texttt{rhog}, \texttt{rhol}, sound speeds \texttt{cg},
\texttt{cl}, compressibility-related coefficients \texttt{kg}, \texttt{kl}, the
driving frequency \texttt{f}, and the PML parameters.  The PML configuration is
selected through \texttt{PMLType}; the current implementation supports
\texttt{rectangle} and \texttt{spherical} configurations.

The PML coefficient fields are generated before the Helmholtz solve by the
companion utility \texttt{setPMLFields}.  This utility reads the same
\texttt{transportProperties} dictionary and writes the scalar fields
\texttt{sigma}, \texttt{SC0}, and \texttt{SC1}, together with the directional
tensor fields \texttt{T0} and \texttt{T1}.  The legacy scalar field
\texttt{TC1} is also written for compatibility.  These fields represent the
polynomial damping profile and the coefficients appearing in the real-imaginary
split of the modified Helmholtz equation.  Separating this
step from the solver makes the PML setup reproducible and allows the generated
fields to be inspected with standard OpenFOAM post-processing tools before the
linear system is assembled.

After the PML fields have been prepared, \texttt{acousticHelmholtzFoam} forms
the block-coupled system for \texttt{Pim} and \texttt{Pre}.  The diagonal block
contains the variable-density Helmholtz operator, while the off-diagonal blocks
contain the PML coupling terms.  In parallel runs, the finite-volume operators
are assembled on the decomposed OpenFOAM mesh and the processor-interface
couplings are inserted into a distributed PETSc matrix.  The resulting global
linear system is solved with PETSc, using the MUMPS direct solver in the tested
configuration.

The primary outputs are the converged real and imaginary pressure fields.  The
solver also writes derived quantities that are useful for acoustic-force
analysis: the pressure amplitude \texttt{pa}, the real and imaginary velocity
components \texttt{Ure} and \texttt{Uim}, the acoustic radiation-pressure-like
field \texttt{pr}, and the momentum-flux tensor \texttt{momFlux}.  A typical
case first generates the mesh and initial fields.  The PML fields are then
written with \texttt{setPMLFields}, after which the Helmholtz solve and the
post-processing of the complex pressure field are performed.

\subsection{Two Phase Formulation}
The modified Helmholtz equation \cref{eq:helmholtzPML} is solved with the open source FVM software OpenFOAM(ESI). For typical two phase system like a droplet in the air, the soundspeed and unpertubated density in liquid and gas domain are constant, which are $c_l,\rho_l$ and $c_g, \rho_g$. Through the interface these material constant will have a jump change. To obtain a one field formulation we use the approach in geometric volume of fluid(gVOF) method and write density and soundspeed as below
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
           \frac{1}{\rho}& = \frac{\alpha}{\rho_l} + \frac{1-\alpha}{\rho_g},  \\
           k^2 &= \omega^2\left(\frac{\alpha}{c_l^2}+\frac{1-\alpha}{c_g^2}\right),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the field $\alpha$ is the liquid volume fraction which takes the value 1 in liquid phase, 0 in gas phase, and some value between 0 and 1 in the interface. 


\subsection{Block coupled approach}
While the Helmholtz equation with large wave number can be easily unstable as solved iteratively or segregately, we rewrite  \cref{eq:helmholtzPML} first in a block coupled equation system
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
    \hat A & \hat B \\
    -\hat B & \hat A
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
    P_{re} \\ P_{im} 
\end{bmatrix}
=0.
\label{eq:blockCoupled}
\end{equation}
With the operators 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \hat A & = \rho_0 \nabla \cdot\left(\frac{1}{\rho_0} \nabla \right) + \nabla\cdot(\mathbf{T}_0\cdot\nabla) +\left(k^2-C_0\right),\\
        \hat B & = \nabla\cdot(\mathbf{T}_1\cdot\nabla) + C_1.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
These operators are constructed with OpenFOAM implicit finite-volume
operators.  The directional rectangular PML is retained by storing
$\mathbf{T}_0$ and $\mathbf{T}_1$ as diagonal volume-tensor fields and
discretizing them with \texttt{fvm::laplacian(T0,P)} and
\texttt{fvm::laplacian(T1,P)}.  Thus each Cartesian damping component can vary
independently, including in edge and corner regions, without modifying the
OpenFOAM core.  The spherical PML currently uses the isotropic tensor form.

While unlike the form-extend software, the block coupled equation 
\cref{eq:blockCoupled} can not be assembled directly within our OpenFOAM-ESI version. So we assemble the equation with the external solver PETSc through its OpenFOAM interface petsc4Foam. The assembled equation is then solved with the MUMPS direct solver also in PETSc. 

\subsection{PML configuration}
The proper choice of the damping coefficient $\sigma$ is of huge importance.  A discontinuous changing of $\sigma$ or too rapid damping in a narrow PML region will lead to numerical reflection or instability if iterativ solver is applied.  In our PML approach the $\sigma$ is a polynomial function of the distance to the open boundary whose order can be adjusted as needed.  At moment, two type of the PML geometry is included in the utilitiy \textbf{setPMLFields}
 \begin{itemize}
     \item Rectangular annulus: we set the type of PML \texttt{PMLType} as \texttt{rectangle}, PML region thickness \texttt{L}, the maximum/minium components of PML region coordinates \texttt{PMLMax=(xMax yMax zMax)} and \texttt{PMLMin=(xMin yMin zMin)}.
     \item Spherical annulus: we set the type of PML \texttt{PMLType} as \texttt{spherical}, PML region thickness \texttt{L}, the maximum/minium radius of PML region coordinates \texttt{Rmax} and \texttt{Rmin}.
 \end{itemize}
The algorithm that detect the open boundary and set up the PML automatically could be a interesting future work. 

Another important thing is, if the damping coefficient is too large or increase too quickly in a narrow band, there will be numeric reflection. In practice, the thickness of the PML is set to about one wave length.

\section{Program structure and usage}
\label{sec:usage}

\subsection{Case setup and input fields}
The solver follows the standard OpenFOAM case structure.  The initial and
boundary data are provided in the time directory, while material and PML
parameters are specified in \texttt{constant/transportProperties}.  The complex
pressure is represented by the scalar fields \texttt{Pre} and \texttt{Pim}.
Acoustic excitation is prescribed through their boundary conditions, for example
as a pressure-gradient condition corresponding to a harmonic wall velocity.

The material distribution is supplied by the volume-fraction field
\texttt{alpha.water}.  A value of zero denotes the gas phase and a value of one
denotes the liquid phase; intermediate values may occur at a resolved interface.
The acoustic properties are read from the entries \texttt{rhog}, \texttt{rhol},
\texttt{cg}, \texttt{cl}, \texttt{kg}, \texttt{kl}, and \texttt{f}.  The same
dictionary defines the PML type and damping profile.  Before the Helmholtz solve,
\texttt{setPMLFields} writes the coefficient fields \texttt{sigma},
\texttt{SC0}, \texttt{SC1}, \texttt{T0}, and \texttt{T1}, which can be
inspected like any other OpenFOAM field.  It additionally writes the legacy
scalar compatibility field \texttt{TC1}.

\subsection{Execution workflow}
A typical case is run by generating the mesh and initial fields, evaluating the
PML coefficient fields, solving the block-coupled Helmholtz system, and then
reconstructing the latest-time results for post-processing.  In parallel this
uses the usual OpenFOAM decomposition workflow with \texttt{decomposePar},
\texttt{acousticHelmholtzFoam -parallel}, and \texttt{reconstructPar}.  The
supplied verification cases provide \texttt{Allrun} scripts that automate these
steps and keep the parameter substitutions reproducible.

\subsection{Output and post-processing}
The primary solver outputs are \texttt{Pre} and \texttt{Pim}.  The solver also
writes the pressure amplitude \texttt{pa}, the real and imaginary acoustic
velocity components \texttt{Ure} and \texttt{Uim}, the radiation-pressure field
\texttt{pr}, and the momentum-flux tensor \texttt{momFlux}.  These fields are
stored in standard OpenFOAM format and can be visualized or sampled with the
usual OpenFOAM and VTK-based tools.

The validation cases include post-processing scripts that compare the numerical
solution with analytical references and generate the data used in
\cref{sec:verification}.  

\section{Verification}
\label{sec:verification}

\subsection{Error norms and convergence assessment}
For verification cases with an analytical reference, the numerical quantity
$y_h(\x_i)$ is compared with the corresponding exact value
$y_{\mathrm{ex}}(\x_i)$ at a common set of sample points $\x_i$.  The relative
discrete $L_2$ and $L_\infty$ errors are defined as
\begin{equation}
    E_2 =
    \frac{
    \left(\sum_i |y_h(\x_i)-y_{\mathrm{ex}}(\x_i)|^2\right)^{1/2}
    }{
    \left(\sum_i |y_{\mathrm{ex}}(\x_i)|^2\right)^{1/2}
    },
    \qquad
    E_\infty =
    \frac{
    \max_i |y_h(\x_i)-y_{\mathrm{ex}}(\x_i)|
    }{
    \max_i |y_{\mathrm{ex}}(\x_i)|
    }.
    \label{eq:verificationErrorNorms}
\end{equation}
For two successive mesh resolutions with characteristic cell sizes $h_1$ and
$h_2$, the observed order of convergence is estimated from either error norm
as
\begin{equation}
    q=\frac{\log(E_{h_1}/E_{h_2})}{\log(h_1/h_2)}.
    \label{eq:verificationObservedOrder}
\end{equation}
The case-specific definitions and normalization of $y_h$ and
$y_{\mathrm{ex}}$ are stated in the corresponding subsections below.

\subsection{Piston radiation}
An axissymmetric model of a rigid cylindrical piston with radius $a$  in an infinite baffle \Cref{fig:piston} is used to varify our frequency domain solver, where the simulation outcome can be directly compared to the known analytical solution. In our setup, the piston vibrate in vertical direction harmonically with the velocity amplitude $U_0$. $\rho_0$ and $c$ is the  density and sound speed of the air. 
\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[scale=0.7]{figures/piston_sketch.png}%
    \caption{Geometry of the piston in an infinite baffle model}
    \label{fig:piston}
\end{figure}
The spherical symmetric acoustic pressure radiated by the piston can be calculate at any point of interest as an integration of the power radiated by a monopole over the surface of the piston 
\begin{equation}
    p(r,t)=i\omega \rho_0 u_0\int_{S}\frac{e^{i(\omega t-kr')}}{2\pi r'}dS.
\end{equation}
Here $\omega$ is the angular frequency,$k=\omega/c$ the wave number , S the surface of the piston and $r$ the distance between the point of interest and the running position on the piston. The open boundary is modeled with spherical type PML boundary condition that has a ring-shape with radius $R_{min}$ and $R_{max}$. The damping function $\sigma$ is decided by the polynomial order $po$ and the largest value at the outer PML boundary $\sigma_{\max}$

Typically the pressure field from the piston source can be divided into the near field and far field. 

For near field, an analytical solution is only available along the symmetric axis. And we have the pressure magnitude on the axis
\begin{equation}
    p(r,0) = 2\rho_0cU_0 \left| \sin\left\{ \frac{1}{2}kr\left[\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{a}{r}\right)^2}\right] \right\} \right|
    \label{fc:nearField}
\end{equation}
For far field with $r>>a$, our pressure is given in spherical coordinate
\begin{equation}
    p(r,\theta,t)= \frac{i\omega a ^2\rho_0U_0}{2r}e^{i(\omega t-kr)}\left[\frac{2J_1(ka\sin\theta)}{ka\sin\theta}\right].
    \label{fc:farField}
\end{equation}
Where $J_1$ is the first order Bessel function.

In our simulation the value of the parameters is given in \Cref{tab:paramsPiston}.

near-field on-axis pressure amplitude is compared with the analytical solution
as shown in \Cref{fig:pistonNearAmp}. The numerical pressure amplitude is
evaluated from the computed complex pressure,
\begin{equation}
    p_h(\x_i)=\sqrt{P_{re}^2(\x_i)+P_{im}^2(\x_i)} ,
\end{equation}
and compared with the analytical amplitude $p_{\mathrm{ex}}(\x_i)$ at sampled

\begin{equation}

    \qquad
    y_{\mathrm{ex}}(\x_i)=\frac{p_{\mathrm{ex}}(\x_i)}{p_0}.
\end{equation}
The resulting errors and observed orders are listed in

For this one-dimensional wave, only $\sigma_x$ is non-zero.  Consequently,
the propagation-direction coefficients $T_{0xx}$ and $T_{1xx}$ are retained
by the directional tensor formulation, while transverse tensor components do
not contribute because the pressure has no transverse gradient.  This provides
a direct verification of the new tensor operators and avoids replacing their
directional contribution by the averaged legacy field \texttt{TC1}.

The numerical real and imaginary pressure components agree closely with the
analytical solution throughout both the physical domain and the PML, as shown
in \Cref{fig:1DLayerdCompare}.  In the physical domain, the relative $L_2$
errors are $1.24\times10^{-3}$ and $1.52\times10^{-3}$ for
$P_{\mathrm{re}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{im}}$, respectively.  Because the acoustic
impedance $\rho c$ of water is much larger than that of air, $R\approx1$ and
the field in medium~1 is approximately a standing wave.  In this limit, the
medium-1 solution can be written as
\begin{equation}
    P(x)=i\rho_1c_1u_0\frac{\cos[k_1(x-L_1)]}{\sin(k_1L_1)},
\end{equation}

At the interface, the outgoing wave is transmitted into medium~2.  Since the
right boundary represents an open domain, no physical reflection should return
from that side.  The field in the undamped part of medium~2,
$L_1<x<L_2$, is therefore a plane travelling wave with constant pressure
amplitude.  Upon entering the PML, the complex coordinate stretching multiplies
the wave amplitude by the attenuation factor
$\exp[-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{h}(x)]$.  For the polynomial damping profile used
in this case, this factor is
\begin{equation}
    \exp\left[
        \frac{\sigma_{\max}(L-L_2)}{(p+1)c_2}
        \left(\frac{x-L_2}{L-L_2}\right)^{p+1}
    \right],
    \qquad L_2<x<L,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{\max}<0$.  Consequently, the amplitude remains constant before
the PML and then decreases smoothly towards the truncated boundary, as
confirmed by \Cref{fig:1DLayerdAmplitude}.  Over $0.20<x<0.34\,\mathrm{m}$,
the numerical and analytical complex pressures differ by only
$1.39\times10^{-3}$ in relative $L_2$ norm, and their pressure amplitudes
differ by $3.45\times10^{-4}$.

The one-dimensional layered-interface case is also used to examine the influence of the PML damping strength.  All physical parameters, the mesh, the PML thickness, and the polynomial order are kept fixed, while only the maximum damping coefficient $\sigma_{\max}$ is varied.  The comparison with the analytical solution is evaluated in the physical part of the domain, excluding the PML and a small offset from the boundaries.  This isolates the error induced by imperfect absorption from the strongly damped layer itself.

\Cref{fig:pmlSigmaSensitivity}.  With the PML coefficients scaled using the

\section{Application example}
\label{sec:application}

\subsection{Height sweep in a single-axis acoustic levitator}
The motivation for developing the frequency-domain solver is to simulate the acoustic field around liquid drops in a single-axis acoustic levitator.  As an application example, we consider the height-sweep configuration of Andrade and Marzo \cite{andrade2019numerical}.  Their COMSOL study evaluates the radiation force on the reflector while varying the transducer-reflector distance $H$ for an empty levitator and for constant-volume drops represented by spheroids with different aspect ratios.

The OpenFOAM case \texttt{WedgeLevitatorDropAlpha} follows the same workflow.  A one-degree wedge sector is used and the resulting force is scaled by the axisymmetric factor $360^\circ/1^\circ$.  The acoustic frequency is $25.25\,\mathrm{kHz}$, the transducer velocity amplitude is $1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and the equivalent spherical drop radius is $1\,\mathrm{mm}$.  The drop is placed at the mid-height of the cavity and is represented by the volume-fraction field \texttt{alpha.water}; for the non-spherical cases the spheroid volume is kept constant while the aspect ratio $a/b$ is varied.  After solving the Helmholtz problem, the reflector force is obtained by integrating the acoustic radiation-pressure field over the reflector patch,
\begin{equation}
    F_n = \int_{\Gamma_r} p_{\mathrm{rad}} \, \normal \cdot \normal_r \, \mathrm{d}S ,
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_r$ is the reflector surface and $\normal_r$ is the mean reflector normal.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/4sweep2.png}
    \caption{Application example: reflector radiation force during a height sweep of the wedge levitator for an empty cavity and constant-volume drops with different aspect ratios, computed with \texttt{acousticHelmholtzFoam}.}

\Cref{fig:heightSweepAspectRatio} shows the resulting force curves.  They reproduce the main trends reported in the COMSOL study of Andrade and Marzo \cite{andrade2019numerical}: the force is strongly amplified near the acoustic resonance, the drop shifts the resonance response, and the force level near the peak increases systematically with the drop aspect ratio.  Because the levitator operates close to resonance, the radiation force is highly sensitive to small changes in geometry, material parameters, acoustic drive, and numerical representation of the drop.  The agreement in resonance scale, curve ordering, and force magnitude is therefore a meaningful application-level comparison.  This example demonstrates that the solver can use the same interface-resolved material description required for two-phase flow simulations while producing acoustic-force trends consistent with the established finite-element reference.
